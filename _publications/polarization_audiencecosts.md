summary: Research on audience costs finds that leaders are punished domestically for not following through on threats in international crises. However, existing studies implicitly assume that individuals all receive common information about the leader's behavior from the same source. This seems a strong assumption in a polarized and fragmented media environment where individuals are often exposed to information from like-minded sources with partisan slant. We identify two ways that media polarization could affect audience costs. First, media polarization might eliminate audience costs, as news sources are viewed as partisan actors rather than purveyors of facts. Second, if citizens are able to ignore the partisan coloring of news coverage and simply focus on the bare facts of the case when evaluating a leader audience costs might survive media polarization. We test these possibilities through three preregistered experiments in the U.S. and find that audience costs are robust to media polarization.
