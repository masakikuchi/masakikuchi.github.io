summary: >-
  Interstate war is said to bind domestic society together, but it also reveals who bleeds for the state. I argue that unequal sacrifice can sustain wartime support while also creating conditions for its erosion. When losses fall disproportionately on marginalized groups, political costs may shift toward the periphery. Yet, this disparity does not foment dissent as long as exposure appears individually chosen. Compulsory call-ups remove this cover, making the state visibly responsible, and existing disparities then generate grievance among burdened groups, weakening support for the leadership and its war. I test this argument using Russia’s September 2022 call-ups, combining fatality records with protest events, survey data, and Telegram posts. After the announcement, pre-existing war burden disparity predicts anti-war protest, greater anger and lower leadership approval among minority respondents, and a rise in anti-war content on Telegram. These findings suggest that the political consequences of war depend not only on how many die, but on who dies relative to whom.
